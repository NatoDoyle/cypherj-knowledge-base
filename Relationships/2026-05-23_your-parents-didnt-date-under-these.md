Be honest. Could your father actually survive modern dating?

Put your father on Tinder in 1987 at the age of 18.

Put your mother on Instagram.

Put both of them inside a world where thousands of people are permanently visible at all times.

Where everyone is comparing their relationship to everybody else’s. Where attention is currency. Where exhaustion is normal. Where people are psychologically *on* all day long.

> **Could they actually survive that environment?**

And I’m not saying this to disrespect older generations. I’m saying this because most people don’t realize older dating advice was built on invisible structures that no longer exist.

Your parents didn’t have better values. They had more stable communities, less comparison, less visibility, less audience pressure, less emotional surveillance, less romantic abundance, less algorithmic temptation, less psychological overload. And they had different economic conditions. That part matters too.

People keep talking about “dating culture” like it appeared out of nowhere. But a lot of dating culture is economic expression.

> The 70s relationship model sounds contradictory now because it existed under different material conditions.

You could still build a life on one income in large parts of America. Communities were more physically rooted. People expected continuity. The future still felt materially reachable. So relationships were allowed to feel heavier socially — because life itself felt more stable economically.

> Then the 80s hit.

The confidence-and-provider model worked because the economy still allowed people to believe in upward motion. And honestly, the 80s were one of the last generations that truly understood scarcity before digital abundance exploded. You could actually disappear into your relationship. You weren’t psychologically competing with thousands of people every day. Your partner wasn’t carrying an entire social marketplace in their pocket at all times.

> Then the 90s hit.

The economy looks strong. The future looks bright. Now the script gets even bolder: *Find your soulmate. Build forever. Retire together. Follow your heart.* But a lot of people inherited visions of the future they themselves were never actually going to receive.

> And by the 2000s, you could already feel the structures straining.

Jobs become less stable. Housing gets harder. Debt rises. Economic pressure increases. But the old relationship script is still running.

### Now “potential” becomes a major dating category.

A woman can hear a man’s job title and immediately start calculating survivability. A man starts tying his worth to economic usefulness because the environment itself is becoming more unstable.

And this is also where resentment starts mutating. Because relationships do not exist outside material reality. A lot of men started blaming women for economic pressures women did not create.

A lot of women started distrusting men who themselves were struggling under collapsing structures. Now everybody is carrying economic anxiety directly into intimacy.

And then social media hits on top of that. Now everybody is continuously visible. Continuously reachable. Continuously comparable. And dating advice never caught up.

> If you were born in the 90s, you probably got taught some version of this: *Man up. Be confident. Lead. Don’t be emotional. Play it cool. Don’t text too fast. Don’t look needy. Pursue her. Women want stability. Just go talk to her.*

Now watch what happens when you put that script inside modern dating.

- “Play it cool” becomes emotional unavailability.
- “Don’t text too fast” becomes anxiety and confusion.
- “Lead” becomes *“you’re controlling.”*
- “Pursue her” becomes *“you’re doing too much.”*
- “Don’t be emotional” turns into two people sitting there silently trying not to scare each other.

> And women got scripts too. *Protect your value. Don’t seem too available. Make him work for it. Watch for red flags. Test consistency. Don’t let him think he has you.*

Now put that inside modern exhaustion and social media paranoia. Now everybody is monitoring everybody. Nobody relaxes. Nobody says what they actually feel.

Everybody’s trying to avoid being hurt, avoid being used, avoid looking stupid, avoid abandonment, avoid pressure, avoid becoming “the problem.”

And eventually the relationship stops feeling like two people meeting each other.

It starts feeling like two nervous systems trying not to trigger collapse.

That’s why modern dating feels so heavy. People think relationships are dying because nobody wants commitment anymore. No. A lot of relationships are dying from exhaustion before betrayal ever even happens.

People are tired. Tired of monitoring every text. Every silence. Every tone shift. Every implication. Every *“what does this mean?”* Every fear of disconnection. Every moment of feeling like they have to manage themselves perfectly inside the relationship.

Some people are not cheating. They’re collapsing.

And this is why the talking stage exists too. Older generations make fun of it because they think it’s immaturity.

But a lot of modern people are unconsciously trying to answer a terrifying question before they attach: ***Can this person psychologically survive modern life with me?***

Because modern relationships require something older systems didn’t demand at this scale: capacity.

### Not game. Not status. Not perfect communication. Not scripts. **Capacity**.

Enough life left in you to stay open after work. After stress. After comparison. After anxiety. After carrying yourself all day long. Enough capacity to tell the truth instead of managing impressions.

Enough capacity to repair after misunderstanding. Enough capacity to stay human while the world keeps trying to turn both of you into performances.

That’s why so many people feel confused right now. They inherited relationship scripts from a world that no longer exists.

And now everybody keeps blaming themselves for failing systems they were never actually built for.
