## A lot of you think you’re good at communication.

You’re actually good at catastrophe prediction.

Your partner gets upset.

And within ten seconds you’ve already imagined the end of the whole relationship.

This comes from the absence of a High-end relationship skill:

**the ability to stay connected to somebody while they’re distressed.**

Because some of you haven’t had arguments in years.

You’ve had a series of emotional emergencies.

Your partner get distressed.

And before they’ve finished their sentence your body has already started predicting the future.

Not the conversation.

The **whole** future.

The breakup.

The cheating.

The abandonment.

And now you’re no longer trying to understand what’s happening.

You’re trying to prevent what you think is about to happen.

That’s the trap.

Because we don’t respond to distress anymore.

We respond to what we think distress **means**.

Have you ever noticed how nobody is allowed to have a bad day anymore?

A bad day becomes:

- a red flag.
- an attachment style.
- a compatibility issue.
- a future prediction.

Somebody gets upset and suddenly everybody is trying to figure out what the **upsetness means** instead of **dealing with the upsetness** itself.

And underneath all of this is one of the saddest beliefs a person can carry.

### The belief that they are a burden.

Not that they’re hurting.

Not that they’re struggling.

That they are a burden.

And that’s strange when you think about it.

Because a burden is weight.

But somehow we’ve learned to turn weight into identity.

> “I need support.”

becomes:

**“I am needy.”**

> “I need help.”

becomes:

**“I am a burden.”**

A lot of people are not afraid of pain.

They’re afraid of becoming a problem while they’re in pain.

#### And once you learn that equation... everything changes.

You stop asking for help.

You sure did stop didn’t you.

You stop reaching.

You start hiding.

You start apologizing for emotions.

You start apologizing for needs.

You start apologizing for existing.

And that’s where this skill comes in.

Co-regulation. Or more accurately, just the thing most of us never learned.

Because a lot of people don’t know what even co-regulation feels like.

You know fixing.

You know explaining.

You know reassuring.

You know defending.

You know diagnosing.

You know withdrawing.

But you don’t know co-regulation.

Some of you have never had somebody stay calm when you were scared.

That was co-regulation.

Some of you have never had somebody stay curious when you were upset.

See what I’m saying?

Some of you have never had somebody stay connected when you were difficult.

Which means every emotional problem feels like an emergency.

And I think this is where it gets heartbreaking.

A lot of people are still open to romance.

They’re just no longer open to accompaniment.

Because the last time they needed somebody they got left alone.

You cried.

They mocked you.

You admitted fear.

They lost respect.

You opened up.

They used it later.

You reached for comfort.

And found criticism.

So now you still believe in love.

You just don’t believe anybody stays when you’re hurting.

And the wound starts showing up everywhere.

> **“Do you want company or space?”**

> **“It doesn’t matter.”**

Except it does matter.

Pain doesn’t just make people reach.

Sometimes pain makes people hide.

Sometimes pain convinces people that needing others is dangerous.

So the very thing they want they make impossible to reach.

Some people ask for connection by making connection impossible.

And before somebody says:

### “What about trauma dumping?”

Good.

Let’s talk about it.

Because trauma dumping is real.

**There are people who repeatedly discharge emotional pain onto others without reflection, boundaries, reciprocity, or responsibility.**

It’s real.

But the internet made a second mistake. Right.

We started calling almost any uncomfortable emotional disclosure trauma dumping.

“I’m scared.”

**Trauma dumping.**

“My marriage is struggling.”

**Trauma dumping.**

“My dad died.”

**Trauma dumping.**

“I’ve been having a hard time.”

**Trauma dumping- Do you get how stupid it is?**

Somewhere along the way, vulnerability itself became suspicious.

And I think the reason is uncomfortable.

A lot of people don’t actually know how to sit beside distress anymore.

So the moment distress appears — they want it **gone**. Flat out gone.

Not because they’re cruel.

Because they’re uncomfortable.

And most people are not afraid of trauma dumping.

They’re afraid of uncertainty.

They don’t know how long this feeling will last.

They don’t want to say that to you though.

They don’t know if they can help.

They don’t want to tell you that though.

They don’t know if they’re enough.

And uncertainty starts feeling heavy.

**So they mistake the uncertainty for the person or themselves for the uncertainty.**

Sometimes the person trying to stop the spiral is more distressed than the person spiraling.

Somebody starts crying.

And before you’ve understood the tears—you’re trying to stop them.

Somebody starts venting.

And before you’ve understood the fear—you’re trying to solve it.

Somebody starts hurting.

And before you’ve encountered the hurt—you’re trying to end the conversation.

And some of you are already asking:

> **“Okay, but what do I say?”**

But that’s the gag.

The fact that you’re looking for the perfect sentence is usually proof you’ve already left co-regulation and entered **fixing**.

Because a lot of you already have the answer.

You’re right.

The solution you’re thinking of might actually work.

But co-regulation is not about whether you’re right.

If somebody is lost in a blizzard and you know the way home, that information is useful.

But before they’re capable of following directions, and most of the time they already are—they need orientation.

You don’t become the blizzard.

You don’t argue with the blizzard.

You don’t diagnose the blizzard.

You don’t pretend the blizzard isn’t there.

You become something they can orient around.

“Yeah. This is rough. I’m here.”

That’s it.

That’s the skill.

And for some of you that feels almost insultingly simple.

Because you’ve spent your whole life believing love meant solving.

But people don’t calm down because you found the perfect answer.

People calm down because they stop feeling alone with the question.

How long do you stay?

**Until they feel encountered.**

Not until the answer appears.

Not until the feeling disappears.

Until they stop carrying it alone.

And this is why every relationship now seems to end with a diagnosis, a content creator, or a therapy recommendation.

Not because therapy is bad.

Therapy can absolutely help somebody understand their wounds.

But understanding a wound and accompanying a wounded person are not the same skill.

We’ve become very good at identifying distress.

And very bad at responding to it.

Some of you have recommended therapy to people you’ve never actually accompanied.

So now let’s get to the part nobody wants to hear.

### A lot of you don’t actually want relationship skills.

You want certainty.

You want a way to never be cheated on again.

Never be abandoned again.

Never waste another year again.

Never be blindsided again.

You’re not looking for co-regulation.

You’re looking for insurance.

But relationships don’t come with insurance policies.

They come with people.

And grief is realizing there is no perfect combination of communication skills, attractiveness, usefulness, loyalty, sacrifice, emotional intelligence, patience, or performance that can remove uncertainty from loving another human being.

The goal was never to become impossible to leave.

#### The goal was to become capable of love despite the fact that people are free.

And I think the thing that breaks my heart the most is how many people learned how to be good partners before they ever learned what it felt like to be accompanied.

You know how to show up.

You know how to care.

You know how to stay.

But if you fell apart tonight… you genuinely wouldn’t know what to do with somebody who stayed.

Because the last time you needed someone you learned that pain was a solitary experience.

It’s the one thing this skill is trying to undo.

Because pain was never supposed to be a solitary experience.

Because the goal was never to make the feeling disappear.

The goal was to make sure nobody had to carry it alone.
