You’ve probably had this conversation before.

Just not this far.

Most conversations begin with answers.

This one will begin earlier than the question.

---

## **Start with life.**

You say, “I have a life.”

Almost immediately someone asks, “Who gave it to you?”

Stop.

Notice what just happened.

That isn’t the first conversation.

That’s already the second.

The first conversation happened so quietly you probably never noticed it.

When did life become the kind of thing that could fit inside the verb **give?**

Long before anyone argued about God.

Long before anyone argued about evolution.

Long before anyone argued about biology.

Life had already become the kind of thing that could be possessed.

**Transferred.**

**Given.**

**Taken.**

The debate inherited an architecture it never investigated.

Then we repair the sentence.

> “I am alive.”

It sounds better.

But look carefully.

Nothing fundamental changed.

Life became a property.

You became the holder.

The room survived.

It simply rearranged itself.

So go further.

We keep talking as though living belongs to someone.

Now stop everything that makes living possible.

Breathing.

Circulation.

Metabolism.

Perception.

Memory.

Continuous exchange between organism and world.

Stop all of it.

Outside of those relations where is the owner?

Don’t answer.

Just notice how quickly your mind wants one.

Hold that feeling.

**Then someone dies.**

Watch what happens.

Not to them.

To us.

For their entire life it was impossible to encounter them as merely a body.

That wasn’t how they appeared.

That wasn’t how they mattered.

That wasn’t how they changed you.

Yesterday the body wasn’t enough to explain their presence.

Today its absence is expected to explain their absence.

Look at the size of that move.

The room changed.

**Then the answers arrive.**

- “They’re in heaven.”
- “They’re gone forever.”
- “They’re still with us.”
- “They became energy.”
- “Soul.”
- “Afterlife.”

Again stop.

Not because one of those answers must be wrong.

Because something happened before every one of them.

When did death become the kind of event that demanded a destination?

Listen to the word.

**Afterlife.**

Almost immediately life becomes a traveler.

The investigation becomes a journey.

But the word never required travel.

That happened inside the room.

---

## **Now leave language for a moment.**

Look at the world.

Seeds become trees.

Trees become soil.

Clouds become rain.

Rivers become oceans.

Stars become heavier elements.

Transformation is everywhere.

You have watched it your entire life.

Then suddenly at the largest question human beings ask transformation is no longer enough.

Now we demand a destination.

Or we demand oblivion.

Notice how quickly certainty arrives.

Not inquiry.

Certainty.

This isn’t an argument for heaven.

It isn’t an argument for annihilation.

It’s an argument for staying inside the investigation long enough to notice how desperately the room wants to finish it.

---

**Now step back.**

Look at what just happened.

Life became a possession.

Living became something owned.

Death became a departure.

Afterlife became a destination.

Every time before the debate the architecture was already there.

That is why I call this the Religion of English.

Not because English tells you what to believe.

Because it quietly teaches you what kinds of questions feel complete.

Then generation after generation we inherit those questions as though they were simply reality itself.

Very few civilizations inherit the ability to see the architecture that made those questions feel inevitable.

And once you mistake the architecture for the world you stop investigating reality.

You begin investigating inside a room you never realized had already been built.
