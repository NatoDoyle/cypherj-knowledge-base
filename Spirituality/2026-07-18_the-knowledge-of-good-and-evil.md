Tell me where good is.

Not what you associate with it.

Not what you were taught to call good.

Where is it?

Because every time we call something good, we speak as though we discovered a property that was already there.

A good relationship.

A good life.

A good person.

A good opportunity.

But you never encounter good first.

You encounter a person.

You encounter relief.

You encounter refuge.

You encounter desire.

You compare what is happening to what happened before.

You imagine what might happen next.

And then, after all of that, cognition says:

Good.

Good comes downstream of almost every form of discernment available.

Then it reaches backward and pretends it was inside the thing from the beginning.

You meet someone.

They make you feel seen.

You trust them.

You experience tenderness.

You imagine a future.

Then you say:

This is a good relationship.

But what exactly did you see?

Did you see what this love would become under grief?

Under money?

Under exhaustion?

Under resentment?

Did you see what staying would cost?

Did you see what leaving would cost?

Did you see every consequence that would come from either decision?

No.

You saw enough of the present to create a prediction.

Then you disguised the prediction as a property.

You did not say:

Based on what I currently know, this relationship appears worth participating in.

You said:

This is good.

Goodness is a forecast spoken as though it were an observation.

---

### **To call something good, you have to imagine that the evaluation is complete.**

The relationship as a whole object.

The person as a whole object.

The decision and everything it will ever produce as one finished thing.

Then you step outside it in your mind and pronounce a verdict.

But you never stepped outside it.

You are still inside the relationship.

Still inside the consequences.

Still inside time.

---

## **Only the false observer sees the whole object.**

The participant sees what keeps happening.

I feel safer here.

I am disappearing here.

We repair this.

We repeat that.

This nourishes me.

This keeps injuring me.

I want to stay.

I need to leave.

Those are recognitions.

They come from inside life.

But “this is good” comes from somewhere else.

An imaginary position above the event.

A position from which you believe you can see what the whole thing means.

What it was worth.

What it will ultimately become.

That position does not exist.

And yet most of us spend our lives trying to observe ourselves from there.

Am I a good person?

Do I have a good life?

Was that a good decision?

Is this a good relationship?

We turn ourselves into objects.

Then we imagine someone standing outside us, evaluating the shape of our existence.

---

## **And once that observer appears, life becomes performance.**

You do not simply help someone.

You watch yourself being helpful.

You do not simply apologize.

You ask what the apology proves about the kind of person you are.

You do not simply live.

You monitor whether your life qualifies as a good one.

This is how someone can suffer inside a good life.

The category belongs to the observer.

The suffering belongs to the participant.

And when the participant speaks, the category is used against them.

You have a good job.

You should be grateful.

You had a good childhood.

Your parents did their best.

You have a good partner.

Nobody is perfect.

Good arrives as a completed verdict and begins overruling the person still living inside the consequences.

Once the relationship is good, its damage becomes an exception.

Once the person is good, their harm requires explanation.

Once the institution is good, the people it injures become errors in the story.

Good does not merely describe.

Good governs attention.

The salary stays in focus.

The exhaustion leaves the frame.

Good tells you what to preserve.

What to excuse.

What to stop investigating.

---

## **And evil does the same thing in the opposite direction.**

Once a person is evil, you no longer have to understand how the harm was produced.

How it was rewarded.

How it became ordinary.

Evil tells you where to stand.

But it does not tell you what happened.

Good and evil depend on the same impossible observer.

The observer who believes it can see what something ultimately is.

But you do not need evil to recognize violence.

You do not need it to recognize coercion.

Domination.

Betrayal.

You do not need to see the completed essence of a person to resist what they are doing.

You need recognition.

You need differentiation.

You need to know what the action produces, who it injures, what permits it, and what must be interrupted.

Calling someone evil ends the investigation too early.

Just as calling a relationship good prevents the relationship from ever becoming visible.

**Good and evil are not the beginning of moral awareness.**

They appear when awareness becomes recursive.

When you no longer only encounter the world.

You begin watching yourself encounter it.

You are not only grieving.

You are watching whether you are grieving correctly.

Before, awareness encounters something.

After, awareness sees itself encountering something.

Then the body can become an object.

The self can become an object.

And life becomes something you imagine can be observed from outside itself.

---

## **There is an ancient story about human beings acquiring the knowledge of good and evil.**

And something strange has already happened inside that story.

When God creates, God looks at creation and calls it good.

But when Adam is brought the animals, Adam is not instructed to call them good.

He is instructed to name them.

God pronounces upon creation from the position of creator.

Adam differentiates from within creation as a participant.

He recognizes.

He distinguishes.

He names what is before him.

But he does not pronounce what the whole of it ultimately is.

**Then God gives Adam a boundary.**

You may eat from every tree except this one.

If you eat from it, you will certainly die.

Most people hear a prediction.

Then the serpent arrives with a competing prediction:

You will not certainly die.

And we imagine that the story has become a debate about who possesses the correct information.

But God does not give Adam a complete image of death.

God gives him a way to navigate what he cannot completely see.

A boundary allows Adam to participate in uncertainty without having to conquer it.

He does not have to know every consequence.

He does not have to imagine death clearly.

He does not have to cross the boundary and survive the consequence before the warning becomes useful.

The frame is stable precisely because Adam is not required to see the whole.

---

## **Then the serpent interrupts it.**

Not necessarily with a lie.

Their eyes do open.

They do acquire the knowledge of good and evil.

And afterward, God says:

The human has become like one of us.

The serpent sees something real.

But the serpent does not stand outside creation either.

The serpent does not possess the entire consequence.

It takes one part of what will happen and renders it at extraordinary resolution.

Your eyes will open.

You will know.

You will become like God.

It displays acquisition.

It does not display concealment.

It displays knowledge.

It does not display the observer that knowledge will install.

It displays likeness to God.

It does not display a limited creature impersonating a position it can never truly occupy.

The serpent does not replace truth with falsehood.

It renders one portion of reality so vividly that the fragment begins to feel complete.

And we still do this.

The salary becomes the job.

The tenderness becomes the relationship.

The worship becomes the church.

The fragment does not have to be false.

It only has to be vivid enough to replace the whole.

Certainty can only be fragmentary from inside the event.

You can become certain about what is visible.

Certain about what you desire.

Certain about what one consequence appears to promise.

But you cannot become certain of the whole without standing outside the whole.

And nobody inside creation stands there.

God gives them a boundary because they cannot see everything.

The serpent gives them a fragment beautiful enough to make them forget they cannot see everything.

It turns the boundary into one claim among competing claims.

God says death.

The serpent says opened eyes.

And now the human is invited to stand above both statements and decide which account of reality deserves authority.

That is already the promise of becoming like God.

Not simply knowledge.

The position of judge.

**And before anyone eats, the mechanism has already begun.**

The woman looks at the tree.

It is good for food.

Pleasing to the eye.

Desirable for gaining wisdom.

But the serpent did not give her a new tree.

It gave her a new position from which to see the same tree.

She sees appetite.

Beauty.

The promise of transformation.

Then those distinctions collapse into a verdict.

Good for food.

Good according to the consequence she can imagine.

But she cannot see every consequence.

She sees what the fruit appears capable of giving her.

She cannot see herself hiding.

She cannot see her body becoming an object beneath an observer.

She cannot see Adam turning her into evidence.

She cannot see the living relationship collapsing into blame.

The selected consequence begins governing the entire encounter.

The transformation does not begin with eating.

It begins when representation reorganizes perception.

---

**Then they eat.**

Their eyes are opened.

And they know they are naked.

But they were already naked.

Nothing happened to their bodies.

What changed was the position from which their bodies were seen.

They did not simply have bodies anymore.

They saw themselves having bodies.

They became visible to themselves as objects.

And then they hid.

Because once you become an object beneath an observer, you do not merely exist.

You appear.

And once you appear, you can be judged.

---

## **Then comes the question: Who told you that you were naked?**

Not:

Who removed your clothes?

Not:

What happened to your body?

Who told you?

This is not merely a question about what Adam knows.

It is a question about the source from which Adam now knows himself.

Who entered between you and your own body?

Who gave you the eyes through which you became visible to yourself as a problem?

Nakedness was not new information.

It was a new relationship to information that had always been true.

Adam was no longer merely naked.

His nakedness now carried a verdict.

“Who told you?” is not a question about anatomy.

It is a source question.

From where are you seeing yourself?

Who taught you to see yourself from somewhere you have never been?

Who installed an observer whose judgment now stands between you and what you are?

But Adam cannot answer the source question.

Because the source no longer feels like a source.

It feels like sight.

The interpretation has disappeared into the fact.

Adam does not say:

I learned to experience my nakedness as shameful.

He says:

I was naked.

The verdict has become reality.

And because Adam is now standing outside himself, he cannot return honestly to what happened.

He can only determine what the object called Adam means.

Good or bad.

Innocent or guilty.

Acceptable or condemned.

God asks:

> Where did this way of seeing come from?

Adam hears:

> Who is at fault?

The question becomes an accusation because every question now passes through the observer.

Before:

- Body.
- Participation.

After:

- Body.
- Representation.
- Observer.
- Verdict.
- Concealment.

---

**And Adam moves it.**

But hiding cannot save an object from judgment.

Once the self has been turned into something accused, there are only two possibilities:

Carry the verdict.

Or move it.

You know this movement.

The moment understanding what happened becomes less urgent than proving what happened does not belong to you.

And Adam moves it.

The woman you gave me.

In one sentence, the person beside him becomes evidence.

She is no longer the one recognized as bone of his bone and flesh of his flesh.

She becomes the object that explains him.

And he does not stop with her.

The woman you gave me.

The blame travels through Eve and reaches toward God.

Because once Adam begins observing himself as an accused object, relation can no longer remain relation.

Someone must become the cause.

Someone must carry what he cannot bear to see attached to himself.

Then Eve moves it too.

The serpent deceived me.

This is not an unrelated moral failure after the knowledge of good and evil.

It is the next cognitive consequence.

Once the self becomes an object under judgment, betrayal becomes self-preservation.

The living relationship fractures into evidence.

The woman.

The serpent.

The God who gave her.

Everyone becomes an object that can be classified, blamed, defended against, and used to redirect the verdict.

The serpent does not create a new world.

It creates a new way of distinguishing the same world.

And by the time blame arrives, the betrayal is already inside the observer.

---

## **God said Adam would certainly die. And Adam does.**

Because Adam was never merely an object called a body.

Adam existed as a relationship.

Breath and ground.

Human and garden.

Human and God.

Human and woman.

Naming what appeared before him.

Participating without pretending to see the whole.

After the fruit, the organism continues.

But Adam cannot remain immediate to his body.

He cannot remain visible before God.

He cannot remain continuous with the woman.

He cannot answer a question without turning it into judgment.

He cannot recognize relation without converting someone into cause.

The body continues.

Adam dies.

The Adam who could receive the boundary is gone.

The Adam who could stand naked without standing outside himself is gone.

The Adam who recognized the woman as flesh of his flesh is gone.

What emerges from behind the trees is biologically continuous with him.

But the form of life through which Adam existed has collapsed.

The only way to test whether Adam would die was to kill the Adam capable of receiving the warning.

The serpent’s fragment was true.

Their eyes opened.

They gained knowledge.

They became like God in the one respect the serpent rendered.

The acquisition was real.

But the recipient died.

---

**That is the knowledge of good and evil.**

Not merely the discovery of moral facts.

The discovery of an imaginary exterior.

The place from which the human believes life can be seen as a completed object.

The place from which a person can be good.

A relationship can be good.

A life can be good.

A world can be good.

But the person living never sees the whole life.

The person living sees what happens next.

What the relationship permits.

What it suppresses.

What repeats.

What changes.

What is repaired.

What is transferred.

What their actions produce.

The living do not encounter good and evil.

We encounter consequence.

We encounter relation.

What nourishes.

What destroys.

What opens.

What closes.

What we will participate in.

What we must refuse.

The knowledge of good and evil did not teach us how to see reality clearly.

It put a bullet hole through your discernment and allowed the deity of yourself.

It allowed the limited self to impersonate the position of God.

Not by knowing everything.

By forgetting that everything else remained outside the frame.

To pronounce upon reality from inside a life it could never stand outside of.

It created the observer who could no longer participate in the world without pretending to stand somewhere beyond it.

And the false observer has been calling things good ever since.
