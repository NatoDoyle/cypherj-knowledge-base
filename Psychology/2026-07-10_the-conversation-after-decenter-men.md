You’ve probably heard it before.

> **“Decenter men.”**

Maybe you’ve heard another version.

“Forget women. Get money.”

Or:

“Don’t lose yourself in your work.”

“Work-life balance.”

“Don’t lose yourself in a relationship.”

“Keep God at the center.”

“Don’t worship the institution.”

At first these sound like completely different conversations.

They’re not.

For decades we’ve kept inventing different versions of the same **advice.**

Different generations.

Different cultures.

Different language.

Same underlying question.

**What belongs at the center of a human life?**

Because the problem was never just men.

Or women.

Or work.

Or money.

Or religion.

The problem has always been what organizes a human life.

I think most of these conversations stop too early.

“Decenter men.”

Okay.

Then what?

Because someone can stop centering men and still be governed by the men who hurt them.

Someone can “forget women” and spend the next twenty years bitter.

Someone can leave a toxic workplace and make productivity their new identity.

Someone can leave religion while remaining organized by resentment toward it.

Changing the object doesn’t necessarily change the organization.

Here’s why I think this happens.

Humans don’t center things because they’re irrational.

They center whatever they believe will finally make life feel okay.

We center the thing we believe will finally settle us.

For some people that’s romance.

For others it’s money.

For others it’s work.

For others it’s status.

For others it’s religion.

Nobody wakes up trying to build a prison.

We mistake the prison for the exit.

And all of us do this.

Every single one of us.

That’s why these conversations feel unfinished.

They’re telling us what to move away from.

Very few tell us what to move toward.

The goal was never the absence of men.

Or women.

Or work.

Or religion.

The goal was freedom.

Freedom to love without disappearing into another person.

Freedom to work without becoming your job.

Freedom to practice faith without confusing it with an institution.

Freedom to encounter people instead of living in reaction to categories.

But there’s another question almost nobody asks.

**What kind of life are you trying to build?**

And then an even harder one.

Are you even trying to build your life?

Or are you trying to inherit someone else’s?

Because a lot of us don’t just center people.

We center images.

The relationship we saw.

The family we admired.

The career everyone applauded.

The lifestyle everyone wanted.

The version of success that photographs well.

So even after becoming free from one center...

we quietly build another prison.

Not because it’s ours.

Because it looked like freedom.

The tragedy is that you can spend your whole life building a life everyone else admires and never build one you can actually inhabit.

You don’t need the life that performs well.

**You need the life you can wake up inside of.**

**The one that lets you recognize yourself.**

The one that isn’t organized around proving something to people who were never going to live your life anyway.

Social media keeps turning all of this into a category conversation.

> “What should women do about men?”

> “What should men do about women?”

But people don’t actually live category-first lives.

They live relational lives.

You don’t date **“men.”**

You date a person.

You don’t heal from **“women.”**

You heal from particular experiences.

You don’t wake up every morning in relationship with an abstraction.

You wake up **in relationship with reality.**

So we’ve been asking the wrong question.

Not:

“What should I decenter?”

But:

> **What is actually worthy of organizing my life?**

Because every human being centers something.

The only real question is whether that center makes you more capable of truth.

More capable of peace.

More capable of love.

More capable of reality.

That’s the conversation after “decenter.”

And that’s the conversation we’ve barely begun.
